Selling to financial institutions: what conventional commercial strategies overlook

A bank or an insurer does not buy like any other company. Approaches that work elsewhere run into decision circuits, due-diligence requirements and a culture of proof that are specific to the sector.

Tyros Group · InsightsOctober 2026  

A technology company, an advisory firm or an investor approaching a bank or an insurer with its usual method (demonstration, trial period, volume of outreach) soon finds that the pace is not its own. It is not slowness for its own sake. In this sector, a poorly prepared purchasing decision engages the institution's responsibility before its supervisor.

A purchase is not a decision, it is a circuit

In a financial institution, the business sponsor does not buy alone. Risk, compliance, information security, procurement, legal and sometimes internal audit each have their say, and each looks at a different risk. A commercial strategy that addresses only the sponsor leaves the most serious objections unanswered.

The circuit (who validates, who can block, in what order) has to be mapped before choosing whom to meet, not afterwards.

Regulation sets the agenda

Priorities often follow the regulatory calendar: operational resilience and control of ICT third-party providers under DORA in the Union, comparable requirements in the United Kingdom, the framing of artificial intelligence. An offer presented as a response to an obligation already identified internally finds a sponsor more easily than one presented as one more innovation.

Regulation also places requirements on the supplier: oversight, audit, exit, subcontracting. These belong in the proposal, not once the discussion is under way.

In financial services, the right conversation comes before the right offer.

Proof before promise

One of the first questions is almost always: "Who else uses it?". For a new entrant, the answer is missing by definition. Proof has to be built differently: an honest framing of what the offer does and does not do, documentation ready for due diligence, exchanges with peers who can confirm the relevance of the subject.

Promises of quantified gains, hard to verify, damage credibility more than they build it.

The same market is never the same market

France, the United Kingdom, Benelux, Switzerland: supervisors, calendars, languages and purchasing habits differ. The same words (resilience, critical third party, high-risk AI) cover neither the same obligations nor the same counterparts from one country to the next. An approach designed for one market is rarely transferable as it stands.

What this changes for the commercial approach

  • Start from the counterpart's agenda (regulatory, risk, transformation) before describing the offer.
  • Map the decision circuit and prepare an answer for each function that validates.
  • Prepare due diligence before it is requested.
  • Prefer a few high-quality conversations to a volume of approaches.
  • Adapt the approach market by market.
